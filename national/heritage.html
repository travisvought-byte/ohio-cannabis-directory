Where weed comes from

Every strain on a shelf today goes back to plants that farmers in particular places selected over generations. These are the places, the landraces and the people who carried the tradition.

Where it all started

A 2021 genome study of 110 cannabis plants from around the world found that cannabis was first domesticated in East Asia in early Neolithic times, about 12,000 years ago. Every hemp and drug variety grown today traces back to an ancestral gene pool that still survives in feral plants and landraces in China. [1][2]

What a landrace is

A landrace is a local variety shaped over generations by the farmers and climate of one place. Botanists trace the two drug types people now call "Sativa" and "Indica" to South Asia and Central Asia. Decades of crossbreeding have since blurred the difference, which is why researchers describe the old, unhybridized landraces as endangered. [7]

China and East Asia

People and traditions: Early Neolithic farmers

Feral and landrace cannabis in China (the ancestral gene pool)

Genetic dating puts the first domestication in East Asia, where some of the oldest surviving cannabis still grows. Selective breeding for fiber or for cannabinoids came much later, around 4,000 years ago. [1][2]

India and the Himalaya

People and traditions: Hindu worshippers of Shiva, sadhus (wandering ascetics), farmers of the Himalayan foothills

South Asian "Sativa" linesHimalayan wild cannabis

India gave the world three words: charas (resin), ganja (flower) and bhang (leaves and seeds, often in drinks). The Atharvaveda names bhanga among five sacred plants that relieve anxiety, though scholars debate whether that meant cannabis. Bhang is offered at the Holi and Shivratri festivals. In 1893 the British government in India appointed the Indian Hemp Drugs Commission, which heard more than 1,100 witnesses and reported in 1894 that moderate use did little harm. Botanists trace the drug type called "Sativa" to South Asia, from where it spread early to Southeast Asia, Africa and the Americas. [3][4][5][6][7]

Afghanistan, Pakistan and the Hindu Kush

People and traditions: Mountain farmers and hashish makers of Afghanistan and northern Pakistan

AfghaniHindu KushAfghan Black (chars)

The drug type called "Indica" traces to Central Asia, including Afghanistan. Afghan hashish, chars, long served local markets and spread beyond them only in the second half of the twentieth century. Afghan genetics became one third of Skunk #1, the hybrid behind much of modern indoor cannabis. [7][8][9]

Morocco's Rif Mountains

People and traditions: Amazigh (Berber) farming communities of the Rif, around Ketama and Chefchaouen

Beldiya (the local kif variety)

Kif has been grown and used in the Rif for centuries; it was widespread by the Saadian dynasty of the 1500s and 1600s. The local beldiya variety has been losing ground to imported hybrids that yield far more hashish. Law 13-21 of 2021 now licenses cannabis for medical, pharmaceutical, cosmetic and industrial use through farmer cooperatives; Morocco's regulator reported 5,765 authorizations in 2026, almost all for cultivation. Recreational use stays outside the law. [10][11][12][13]

Law today: Medical and industrial cultivation licensed since 2021; recreational use illegal.

Lebanon's Bekaa Valley

People and traditions: Farming families and clans of the Bekaa, many growing on land passed down through generations

Lebanese red and blond hashish

Hashish production was banned in 1926 under the French Mandate, but cultivation in the Bekaa never stopped. In April 2020 Lebanon became the first Arab country to legalize cannabis cultivation for medical and industrial use, yet six years on some Bekaa families are still growing outside the law. [14][15]

Law today: Medical and industrial cultivation legal on paper since 2020; recreational use illegal.

South Africa and southern Africa

People and traditions: Khoikhoi, San and Bantu-speaking peoples; Mpondo farmers of the Eastern Cape

Durban PoisonMpondo landrace

The word dagga comes from the Khoikhoi word dacha. Cannabis is thought to have reached Africa with Arab or Indian traders and was in wide use among Khoisan and Bantu peoples before Europeans settled the Cape in 1652; Jan van Riebeeck noted it in his journal in 1658. South Africa's landraces include Durban Poison, known worldwide, and the Mpondo landrace of the Eastern Cape, whose small farmers risk being left out of the new legal market. [16][17][18][19]

Law today: Private adult use decriminalized by the Constitutional Court in 2018; the 2024 Act awaits final regulations.

Jamaica and the Caribbean

People and traditions: Indian indentured laborers and their Indo-Jamaican descendants; the Rastafari

Lamb's Bread and other Jamaican sativas

Between 1845 and 1917 about 35,000 indentured workers came from India to Jamaica, bringing their religious traditions and cannabis with them. The word ganja is Hindi. The Rastafari later took up ganja in their spiritual practice, and one theory traces dreadlocks to Hindu holy men who came over as indentured laborers. [20][21][22]

Law today: Up to 2 ounces decriminalized since 2015; licensed medical herb houses.

Thailand and Southeast Asia

People and traditions: Farmers of Thailand and neighboring Laos

Thai (Thai stick)

Thai buds tied to slender sticks became famous in the late 1960s and 1970s. US troops stationed in Thailand during the Vietnam War bought them cheaply and started shipping them home, building the first wave of demand in the West. [23][24]

Law today: Flower is prescription-only since 2025.

Mexico and Colombia

People and traditions: Mountain farmers of Guerrero and Michoacán in Mexico and the Sierra Nevada de Santa Marta in Colombia

Acapulco GoldMichoacánColombian (Santa Marta) Gold

These were the names Americans knew in the 1960s and 1970s, each named for where it grew. Acapulco Gold and Colombian Gold, crossed with Afghan genetics, produced Skunk #1, which made uniform, fast-flowering indoor cannabis possible. [9]

Law today: See the Mexico page.

Hash: the oldest concentrate

Long before extraction labs, people collected the resin glands (trichomes) of the plant by hand or by shaking dried plants over sieves. Most traditional hash still comes from one of two methods.

Hand-rubbed (charas)

India and Nepal, also Afghanistan

Resin is rubbed off living plants by hand. North India and Nepal have a long tradition of making charas this way. [25][26][27]

Sieved (dry sift)

Morocco, Lebanon, Afghanistan, Pakistan

Dried plants are beaten or shaken over fine screens and the powder that falls through is pressed into blocks. In Afghanistan, sieved resin is also placed in heated mortars and pounded with a heavy object. Sieved hash is much easier and faster to make than hand-rubbed charas. [25][27][28]

Trade history

Afghanistan, Pakistan, Lebanon, Morocco

Afghan and Pakistani sieved hash reached Europe along the 1960s and 1970s overland "hippie trail", with Lebanon the other main source. Morocco was the last of the major hash nations to produce enough to export, starting in the late 1960s, and its production grew while wars disrupted supply from Lebanon and Afghanistan. [29][25]

Modern solventless forms such as ice-water hash and rosin are explained in Cannabis 101.
