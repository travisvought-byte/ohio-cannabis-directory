Does Medicaid, Medicare or insurance pay for medical cannabis?

Short answer: patients pay for dispensary cannabis themselves. What can change is the cost of the card, and a few cannabis medicines are ordinary prescriptions. Each answer below comes from the agency’s own page.

Does Medicaid pay for medical cannabis?

No.

  • Maryland’s Cannabis Administration states that the cost of medical cannabis is not reimbursable by any private or public insurance plan, Medicare or Medicaid, or through veterans’ covered plans. [1]
  • State medical cannabis programs that address the question say no. Mississippi’s program states that neither Medicaid nor private insurance will pay for medical cannabis or medical cannabis products. [2]
  • Being on Medicaid can still lower the cost of the card itself in some states. See the card fee help below.

Does Medicare pay for medical cannabis?

Not for dispensary cannabis. A small hemp pilot exists.

  • Maryland’s Cannabis Administration states that the cost of medical cannabis is not reimbursable by any private or public insurance plan, Medicare or Medicaid, or through veterans’ covered plans. [1]
  • Since April 1, 2026, care organizations in two Medicare Innovation Center models (ACO REACH and the Enhancing Oncology Model) may give eligible patients up to $500 a year of hemp products. A third model, LEAD, joins on January 1, 2027. [3]
  • The pilot covers only federally legal hemp products with no more than 0.3% delta-9 THC, and no inhalable products. CMS says it does not override the Controlled Substances Act or authorize Schedule I substances. [3]
  • Medicare does not pay for the products, and patients cannot buy retail products and send in receipts. Only patients already aligned to a participating organization can receive them, after a physician decides it is appropriate. [3]

Does private health insurance pay?

Generally no.

  • Maryland’s Cannabis Administration states that the cost of medical cannabis is not reimbursable by any private or public insurance plan, Medicare or Medicaid, or through veterans’ covered plans. [1]
  • Alabama’s Medical Cannabis Commission states that health insurers are not required to cover the cost of medical cannabis. [4]
  • Mississippi’s program states that private insurance will not pay for medical cannabis or medical cannabis products. [2]
  • Your own plan’s documents are the final word. Ask the plan before you assume either way.

Does the VA pay, and does using it affect VA benefits?

The VA does not pay. Veterans are not denied benefits for using it.

  • The VA says veterans will not be denied VA benefits because of marijuana use, and encourages veterans to discuss marijuana use with their VA providers. [5]
  • The VA says its clinicians may not recommend medical marijuana or complete the forms for state programs, its pharmacies may not fill prescriptions for it, and the VA will not pay for it from any source. [5]
  • Some states cut or waive the card fee for veterans. See the card fee help below.

Are any cannabis medicines covered like regular prescriptions?

Four FDA-approved drugs are prescription medicines.

  • The FDA has approved one cannabis-derived drug, Epidiolex (cannabidiol), and three synthetic cannabis-related drugs: Marinol and Syndros (dronabinol) and Cesamet (nabilone). They are available only with a prescription from a licensed healthcare provider. [6]
  • The FDA has not approved cannabis itself, or any other cannabis or CBD product on the market, to treat any disease or condition. [6]
  • Because those four are prescription drugs, ask your doctor and your plan’s pharmacy benefit whether one is covered for you.

Can I deduct medical cannabis as a medical expense?

The IRS says no.

  • IRS Publication 502 says you cannot include in medical expenses amounts you pay for controlled substances, such as marijuana, that are not legal under federal law, even if state law has legalized them. [7]

Card fee help by state

States that lower or waive the patient card fee for people on Medicaid or with low income, and for veterans, among the state programs reviewed so far.

  • Arizona$75 if you are eligible for SNAP and submit current documentation; $275 for a minor patient application with SNAP. [8]
  • CaliforniaMedi-Cal beneficiaries get a 50 percent reduction (no more than $50). Fees are waived for indigent patients in the County Medical Services Program. [9]
  • ColoradoThe fee can be waived for household income at or below 185% of the federal poverty level, shown by a tax return. [10]
  • IllinoisVeterans with an honorable or general discharge who are eligible for VA services get a reduced application fee, with a copy of their DD-214. [11]
  • Mississippi$15 instead of $25 for Medicaid participants. Waived for disabled veterans and disabled first responders with documentation. [2]
  • OhioIndigent status (for example SSDI or SSI enrollment) and veteran status are recorded at registration. Send proof to the Division if your physician did not mark it. [12]
  • Oklahoma$20 instead of $100 with proof of Medicaid (SoonerSelect) or Medicare enrollment, or 100% disabled veteran status. [13]
  • OregonReduced fees with current proof: $60 with SNAP, $50 with the Oregon Health Plan, $20 with SSI, $20 for veterans. No fee for veterans with a service-connected total disability rating of at least 50 percent and a discharge under other than dishonorable conditions. [14]
  • PennsylvaniaPeople in Medicaid, PACE/PACENET, CHIP, SNAP or WIC may qualify for a no-cost card instead of paying $50. [15]
  • West VirginiaThe $50 fee can be waived for household income below 200% of the federal poverty level. [16]

A state missing from this list has not been reviewed for fee help yet, or its regulator page did not state any. Checked November 17, 2026. Not medical, legal or tax advice.
