Laws side by side

Limits on how many businesses can be licensed

Does the state cap the number of cannabis businesses?

Ohio

No more than 400 dispensaries may operate at one time. The Division of Cannabis Control sets the number of cultivator licenses, considering population and the number of patients and adult consumers. One person may hold no more than one cultivator license, one processor license and eight dispensary licenses. New dispensaries may not be licensed or relocated within one mile of another dispensary.

Ohio Revised Code 3796.05 · in effect March 20, 2026 · checked October 21, 2026 [1]

Michigan

No statewide limit on the total number of licenses. The state must approve an application that meets the legal requirements; municipalities may limit establishments locally. Ownership limits apply, such as interests in no more than five growers, though the state may allow exceptions by rule.

MCL 333.27959 (Initiated Law 1 of 2018) · checked October 21, 2026 [5]

Oregon

License numbers are capped per capita by license type under House Bill 4121 (2024). New licenses have been on hold since 2022; a license type reopens only when its active licenses fall below the population ratio. Regulators were discussing a limited reopening in September 2026.

Oregon House Bill 4121 (2024) and Oregon Liquor and Cannabis Commission rules · checked October 21, 2026 [10][9]

Limits on growing space

How much can a licensed grower cultivate?

Ohio

Level I cultivators may have up to 100,000 square feet of cultivation area and Level II cultivators up to 15,000 square feet, including any expansions the Division of Cannabis Control approves.

Ohio Revised Code 3796.18 · in effect March 20, 2026 · checked October 21, 2026 [2]

Michigan

Grower licenses are set by plant count: Class A up to 100 plants, Class B up to 500 and Class C up to 2,000. There is no statewide limit on how many grower licenses are issued.

MCL 333.27959(2) · checked October 21, 2026 [5]

Lessons from other markets

What happened elsewhere, why it happened, and whether Ohio has the same conditions.

Oversupply and falling prices

When licenses and growing capacity expand faster than demand, prices can fall sharply and many businesses close.

What happened in Michigan

  • The average price of an ounce of adult-use flower fell from more than $512 in January 2020 to $117 in August 2022, according to state data. [6]
  • It kept falling: $95.08 in December 2023, $69.20 in December 2024 and $58.20 in December 2025. [8]
  • Plants under cultivation rose from about 400,000 to nearly 1.5 million in the year before October 2022, and reached 3.2 million by June 2025. [6][7]
  • Adult-use sales fell in 2025 for the first time since legalization, to $3.17 billion from $3.27 billion in 2024. [8]
  • More than 550 dispensaries and cultivators have closed since adult-use sales began in December 2019, including about 191 grow operations. [8]
  • TerrAscend announced in June 2025 that it would leave Michigan, closing 20 stores and four cultivation facilities. [7]

Why it happened

  • No statewide limit on the number of licenses: the state must approve qualifying applications, and changing the voter-approved law requires a three-quarters vote of the legislature. [5][6]
  • Growing capacity expanded much faster than sales. [6][7]

Does Ohio share these conditions?

No statewide limit on licenses: Ohio does not share this condition

Ohio has limits Michigan lacks: a 400-dispensary cap, a one-mile spacing rule for new dispensaries, one cultivator license per person, and a cultivator count set by the Division of Cannabis Control. Ohio had 37 cultivators (23 Level I and 14 Level II) as of October 16, 2026. [1][3]

Rapid growth in growing capacity: Ohio partly shares this condition

Growing space is capped per license, but cultivators may expand up to those caps with Division approval, so total capacity can still grow. The directory has not found a published figure for total growing space in use. [2]

Ohio so far

  • Ohio's average adult-use price fell from $9.40 a gram when sales began in August 2024 to $6.80 in late January 2025. [4]
  • The Division of Cannabis Control reported an average of $6.55 a gram for plant sales in the week of October 4 to 10, 2026. [3]
  • These two figures may not be calculated the same way: the 2025 figure is an adult-use analysis of state data, and the 2026 figure is the Division's average for all plant sales. [4][3]
Directory assessment · analysis, not fact
Ohio will see a Michigan-scale price collapse within the next two years.Unlikely · moderate confidence

Ohio limits the conditions that drove Michigan's collapse: the number of cultivators is set by the Division, dispensaries and ownership are capped, and growing space has a ceiling. Ohio prices fell quickly after adult-use sales began but have changed little since early 2025. Confidence is moderate because total growing space in use is not published, provisional dispensaries are still opening, and the price figures are not fully comparable.

Ohio prices will drift meaningfully lower over the next two years rather than hold near current levels.Roughly even chance · low confidence

Prices in maturing markets have generally fallen over time, and Ohio's early drop fits that pattern. But Ohio's average has been nearly flat since early 2025, and only two price points have been reviewed so far. Confidence is low until a longer run of the Division's weekly data is reviewed.

Assessed October 21, 2026 · next review by January 21, 2027

Signals to watch

  • The Division's weekly average price per gram
  • The number of cultivators and approved cultivation-area expansions
  • How many provisional dispensaries open (47 as of October 16, 2026)
  • Any change to the limits in Ohio Revised Code 3796.05 or 3796.18

How other states responded

  • Oregon: Oregon stopped issuing new licenses for applications received after January 1, 2022, under House Bill 4016. In 2024, House Bill 4121 set per-capita caps by license type; a type reopens only when active licenses fall below its population ratio. [9][10]
  • Michigan: Michigan's largest operators asked lawmakers in 2022 for a moratorium on new grower licenses. Because the voter-approved law calls for unlimited licenses, a change needs a three-quarters vote; the sources reviewed do not show that a moratorium passed. [6]
